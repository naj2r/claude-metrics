## Substantive content

### What vote #60 was

**Title (German, 1903)**: *Bundesgesetz vom 10. Oktober 1902 betreffend den schweizerischen Zolltarif* — Federal Law of 10 October 1902 concerning the Swiss customs tariff.

**Type**: Optional referendum (`rechtsform == 2`) on legislation passed by the Federal Assembly. Triggered by 30,000+ signatures, per the 1874 Constitution's optional-referendum mechanism.

**Date**: 25 October 1903.

**National outcome**: Passed (`annahme == 1`).

**What the law did**: Established the new federal customs tariff schedule effective 1 January 1906. The schedule comprehensively raised import duties on agricultural products, manufactured goods, and beverages. For wine specifically, foreign wine imports were subject to duties in the range of CHF 8-25 per 100 kg depending on category and bottling, protecting Swiss producers (especially Vaud, Valais, Geneva, Neuchâtel) from price competition with French, Italian, and Spanish imports.

**Historical context**: The 1903 tariff debate occurred against the backdrop of European economic-nationalist consolidation in the late 19th and early 20th centuries (Bismarck's tariff revival 1879; French Méline tariff 1892; US McKinley/Dingley/Payne-Aldrich 1890/1897/1909; Swiss tariff increases 1891 and 1903). The 1903 Swiss tariff was a continuation of this trend. Producer-group support for higher tariffs is the textbook Stigler/Becker prediction.

### Vote #60 in the falsification panel

From the Round-2 t13_placebo_panel.tex and the regression rows tagged `panel_anr60`:

| Spec | Vineyard coef | SE | p-value (analytical HC3) |
|------|---:|---:|---:|
| #60 customs tariff 1903 | **+737.34** | 754.03 | 0.328 |
| #65 food law 1906 | +1285.58 | 603.82 | 0.045 |
| #68 absinthe ban 1908 | +484.40 | 200.41 | 0.024 |

The coefficient on #60 is the second-largest positive in the 15-vote panel (after #65 at +1286), but its standard error is large enough that it's not statistically significant at conventional levels. This is consistent with two interpretations:

**Interpretation A (wine-industry rent-seeking, conservative)**: Vineyard cantons did support the 1903 tariff because it included wine-protective duties, but the support was diffused across many other tariff items (not all of which mattered to wine cantons), so the coefficient reflects a real-but-noisy positive effect. The lack of statistical significance reflects N=25 power limitations (similar to the H3/H6 framing from Task B), not the absence of an effect. Under this interpretation, #60 IS a third member of the wine-industry rent-seeking cluster.

**Interpretation B (chance positive correlation)**: Vineyard cantons may have voted for the 1903 tariff for reasons unrelated to wine (e.g., general protectionism, political alignment with the federal government of the day, or canton-specific economic interests). The coefficient is positive by chance, and the lack of significance reflects the absence of a real wine-industry effect. Under this interpretation, #60 is a noisy null-effect placebo, not a comparator.

The data alone cannot distinguish A and B at N=25. The historical record on the wine-protection content of the 1903 tariff would help: if the per-commodity tariff schedule shows substantial wine-import duties, Interpretation A is more plausible; if not, Interpretation B is more plausible.

The round-2 handoff `07_taskC4_rank_recompute.md` Pitfall #1 explicitly anticipated this scenario:

## Why this matters for the paper

1. **The Stiglerian-capture story may be 3-vote, not 2-vote.** The round-1 second headline (`progress_2026-04-30_1830_foodbev.md`) framed the regulatory-capture pattern as "wine cantons supported #65 (food law, 1906) and #68 (absinthe ban, 1908) — two sequential acts in a single rent-seeking story." If vote #60 (1903 customs tariff) is wine-industry-relevant, the pattern is "tariff protection (1903) → product-purity regulation (1906) → product-elimination (1908)" — a 5-year sequence of wine-industry rent-seeking via three different regulatory channels.

2. **The strict-rank-#1 framing is wrong.** The original "absinthe ranks #1 of 14 in the true-placebo set" framing is empirically false. The honest framing is "absinthe ranks #2 of 14, with the only exceeding vote being #60 (1903 customs tariff, p=0.328 not significant), which is plausibly wine-industry-relevant." This is more transparent and methodologically defensible.

3. **The N=25 power limitation extends to #60's significance.** Just as H3 and H6 were null at N=25 due to power constraints (per `progress_2026-05-01_2030_bbtests.md`), the #60 coefficient at p=0.328 is consistent with both "real but small effect" and "null effect." The data cannot resolve this; only historical evidence on the tariff's wine-protective content can.

4. **The paper text needs a one-paragraph acknowledgment** of #60 in the falsification section, with explicit reference to the historical interpretation and the limit on what the data can say.

## Mechanism / interpretation

If #60 is genuinely wine-industry-relevant (Interpretation A), the mechanism is straightforward Stiglerian: producer groups support regulation that disadvantages competitors. In 1903, the competitor was foreign wine imports; in 1906, the competitor was domestic wine adulteration and substitute beverages; in 1908, the competitor was absinthe specifically. The wine producer coalition reassembled across three votes spanning five years to obtain three distinct regulatory protections.

If #60 is a chance positive correlation (Interpretation B), the wine-industry pattern is the 2-vote pattern from round 1 (just #65 and #68), and #60's positive direction is statistical noise.

The data at N=25 cross-section cannot distinguish these. The paper should present the rank #2 of 14 result honestly, mention #60 as plausibly wine-industry-relevant, and lean on the historical interpretation rather than the statistical inference.

## Caveats / open questions

- **The historical interpretation is the load-bearing claim**, not the regression coefficient. At p=0.328 with N=25, the data cannot support a strong inference; the case for #60 being wine-industry-relevant rests on the historical record of the 1903 tariff's wine-protective content, which has not been verified in this project.

- **Should #60 be reclassified as a comparator?** If we treat #60 as analogous to #65 (move it out of the placebo set), the true-placebo set shrinks to 12 votes and absinthe becomes #1 of 13 (permutation-style p = 1/13 ≈ 0.077). This is the question for user/strategist decision. Arguments for reclassification: substantive a priori case, retains the strict #1 framing. Arguments against: coefficient not significant, weaker historical evidence than for #65, conservative falsification benchmark requires keeping ambiguous votes in the placebo set.

- **The 1903 tariff's actual wine-import duties need verification.** I (the coder) have asserted that the 1903 tariff "included substantial duties on foreign wine imports" based on general historical knowledge of the European tariff context. This needs verification against primary sources (the actual tariff schedule) before the paper makes any claim about #60's wine-industry relevance.

- **#60 was not flagged in the round-1 falsification work.** The round-1 framing identified only #65 as a non-treatment positive coefficient; #60 was buried in the histogram of "placebo distribution." The round-2 rank-recompute is what surfaced it. This suggests other latent positives may exist in the histogram that weren't flagged because they didn't exceed absinthe — but they are part of the falsification distribution.

- **Round-1 commit b9f2da3** introduced the #65 reclassification language. A subsequent commit may need to add #60 reclassification language if the user/strategist decides Interpretation A is the right reading. Until then, the round-2 documentation treats #60 as a "soft" wine-relevant placebo (acknowledged but not reclassified).
