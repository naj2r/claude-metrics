## Headline (1 paragraph)

Switzerland's federal agricultural subsidy budget contains a dedicated "Weinbau, Traubenverwertung" (Viticulture and grape processing) line item that **first appears in 1908 with 133,000 CHF**, the same year as the 5 July 1908 absinthe-ban referendum. The line is BLANK from the start of the federal subsidy series in 1866 through 1907. After 1908 it rises rapidly: 237K CHF (1909), 277K CHF (1910), then 115K-251K CHF annually through 1915. If the line item reflects an actual newly-created federal subsidy program rather than a categorical reclassification of pre-existing spending, the finding extends the Stiglerian-capture interpretation across both directions of industrial policy: the wine industry secured both an anti-competitor *regulation* (the 1908 absinthe ban, vote #68) and a positive *transfer* (the 1908 federal viticulture subsidy) in the same calendar year. Disambiguating between the two interpretations requires consulting Brugger (1968) — the cited source for the I.33 series — to determine whether the 1908 line item represents (a) actual program creation or (b) a chart-of-accounts reorganization of prior spending.

## Why this matters for the paper

### If the 1908 line item reflects an actual newly-created federal viticulture subsidy program

This becomes a **third headline finding** alongside:

1. **Headline 1 (Simpson sign-flip)**: vineyard_per_cap on yes_pct flips from negative (bivariate) to positive (KEY spec with French + Catholic shares). The conditional effect is +484, p=0.024.

2. **Headline 2 (Stiglerian capture across multiple votes)**: vineyard cantons supported #65 (1906 food-purity law, +1286) and #68 (1908 absinthe ban, +484), but were null on #63 (1903 alcohol-trade regulation, −52). Industry support for regulation when and only when it benefits the industry competitively.

3. **Headline 3 (paper-completing capture: regulation + subsidy in 1908)**: The same 1908 calendar year that produced the absinthe ban also produced the first dedicated federal viticulture subsidy line item. This is the canonical empirical signature of a captured industrial-policy package — wine producers received both an anti-competitor regulation AND a positive transfer in the same year. The pattern fits Stigler's (1971) prediction that captured industries acquire "regulation [that] is designed and operated primarily for [their] benefit."

The third headline would let the paper move from "wine industry shaped a 1908 regulation" to "wine industry shaped a 1908 *industrial policy program*" — a stronger claim that touches both sides of the regulatory ledger.

### If the 1908 line item reflects only a categorical reclassification of pre-1908 spending

The finding shrinks substantially. Pre-1908 federal viticulture subsidies may have existed but been embedded in broader categories like "Bodenverbesserungen" (soil improvements, which includes vineyard terracing), "Other subsidies", or "Associations and exhibitions" (which would include viticultural associations). The 1908 emergence then reflects only the federal accounting office's decision to break out viticulture as its own line — a categorical reporting decision, not a substantive policy change.

In this interpretation, the 1908 timing is coincidental (or weakly causal — the absinthe-vote attention may have prompted the accounting separation). The finding remains worth a footnote in the paper but not a third headline.

### Why the disambiguation matters substantively

Both interpretations are consistent with the broader Stiglerian story (the wine industry was a politically organized constituency capable of moving federal policy), but they have different implications for how strongly the absinthe ban can be characterized as part of a deliberate policy package vs. a one-off vote that happened to coincide with an unrelated accounting change. The strong interpretation (program creation) makes the absinthe ban the visible regulatory half of a coordinated 1908 wine-industry victory; the weak interpretation (reclassification) leaves the absinthe ban as the standalone main event with the subsidy line as decorative coincidence.

## Mechanism / interpretation

The Stiglerian rent-seeking story (canonical statement: Stigler 1971, "The Theory of Economic Regulation," Bell J Econ 2(1)) predicts that captured industries acquire two complementary forms of favorable government action:

1. **Anti-competitor regulation** — restrictions on substitutes, entrants, or low-cost competitors. Examples in our data: 1906 Lebensmittelgesetz cracked down on adulterated wine and substitutes; 1908 absinthe ban eliminated a specific spirits competitor.

2. **Positive transfers** — direct subsidies, tax preferences, infrastructure investment. Example candidate: 1908 federal viticulture subsidy line.

Industries that achieve only one half of this (regulation only, or subsidies only) are partial captures. Industries that achieve both are full captures — and the joint observation is much stronger evidence of underlying political organization than either alone.

If the 1908 viticulture subsidy is a real program creation, the wine industry achieved full capture in a single calendar year:
- **Anti-competitor**: vote #65 (1906) → vote #68 (1908) → both wine-industry-favorable regulations passed
- **Positive transfer**: 1908 viticulture subsidy line begins (133K CHF, growing rapidly thereafter)

This kind of joint pattern is documented qualitatively for many regulatory episodes but rarely supported by clean cross-vote quantitative evidence. Switzerland's combination of (a) detailed federal subsidy budget records, (b) referendum-based regulatory adoption with cantonal vote counts, and (c) a small federation where one industry can plausibly move both at once, makes the joint observation unusually clean if it holds up.

The alternative interpretation (categorical reclassification) is institutionally plausible — federal accounting reorganizations are common — but would require Brugger 1968 to document the reclassification rather than a budget-line creation.

## Caveats / open questions

- **Disambiguation requires Brugger 1968.** The HSSO source line in I.33a is "Quelle: Brugger 1968" (no fuller bibliographic detail visible in the file; likely Brugger's book on Swiss agricultural policy history). A 1-2 hour library lookup of Brugger 1968 should resolve whether 1908 represents (a) actual program creation or (b) categorical reclassification. Until that's done, the paper-text framing should be cautious.

- **HSSO does NOT have canton-level subsidy data for the 1900-1908 window.** I.33b (federal+cantonal subsidies, more category detail) and I.33c (cantonal subsidies aggregate) are also national-only. No per-canton viticulture subsidy series exists in HSSO. Cantonal archives (Geneva, Vaud, Valais, Neuchâtel cantonal budget records) would be needed for a per-canton check. Out of scope for this paper.

- **N=8 post-1908 years (1908-1915) is small** for any time-series claims about the trajectory. The pattern is more about the BREAK at 1908 than about post-1908 growth.

- **Confound with WWI**: 1915 sharp drop (43K) almost certainly reflects WW1 budget reallocation rather than a substantive change in viticulture policy. Any claim about "viticulture subsidy declined after 1908" must control for the war effect.

- **133K CHF is small in absolute terms** — about 4% of the 1908 federal agricultural budget and a tiny fraction of overall federal spending. The substantive significance is the *categorical specificity* (a dedicated wine line), not the magnitude.

- **Alternative subsidy categories that may have absorbed pre-1908 viticulture spending**: B (Bodenverbesserungen / soil improvements, includes vineyard terracing); K (Vereine und Ausstellungen / associations and exhibitions, may include viticultural associations); L (Übrige / other). Brugger 1968 would clarify whether and how these absorbed wine-related spending.
